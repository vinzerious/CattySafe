## Users
- **Primary users (confirmed from brief):** Everyday car drivers in Thailand and globally who want a fast, reliable way to make sure no stray or neighborhood cats are hiding under their chassis, atop their tires/wheel wells, or inside their warm engine bay before starting the car and driving off.
- **Situation & job (inferred from brief):** Standing beside a parked car (often in a dim garage, carport, or street, sometimes in a hurry or rainy/cool weather when cats seek engine warmth) needing a 15-to-30-second pre-drive acoustic sweep that flushes hidden cats out safely without requiring the driver to crawl on the ground.

## Product Purpose
PaiPai-ChewChew prevents tragic injuries and deaths of cats hiding under vehicles or inside engine compartments. It combines real-time Web Audio procedural sound synthesis tailored to feline auditory physiology with the proven Japanese "Neko Ban Ban" (Knock-Knock for Cats) pre-drive safety protocol. Success means a driver can trigger an effective, anti-habituation acoustic sequence in one tap before entering the car, coax out frozen kittens when needed, and verify all four vehicle hazard zones in under 30 seconds.

## Positioning
Unlike generic "ultrasonic pest repeller" apps that play static 23–25 kHz tones (which smartphone speakers physically filter out due to ~18–20 kHz hardware roll-off) or single-loop MP3s that cats quickly ignore, PaiPai-ChewChew synthesizes multi-band acoustic patterns matched to both **feline peak auditory sensitivity (500 Hz – 19.5 kHz smartphone-deliverable ceiling)** and **feline ethology**:
1. **Anti-Habituation Deterrent Sweep** (13.5–19.5 kHz FM sweep, aggressive feline hiss sibilance, metallic shaker transients, and canine warning growl/bark harmonics) to flush cats from under the chassis and wheel wells.
2. **Coax / Lure Reversal Mode** (kitten contact calls, prey squeaks, and food-can transients) specifically designed for the "Engine Bay Paradox"—where a terrified cat trapped deep under the hood freezes instead of fleeing and must be lured outward rather than panicked deeper into fan belts.

## Operating Context
- Used primarily one-handed on a smartphone while walking around a parked vehicle (hood, front wheel wells, undercarriage, rear exhaust/tires).
- Frequently used in low-light environments (underground car parks, early morning commutes, night parking) where high-contrast legibility and immediate tactile controls matter.
- Smartphone speakers have physical acoustic roll-off above ~18–20 kHz; all high-frequency deterrence must operate within the 13.5 kHz – 19.5 kHz deliverable band paired with broadband 3–12 kHz transients.

## Evidence on Hand
- Feline audiogram research (Heffner & Heffner, 1985): Cat hearing spans ~45 Hz to 64–79 kHz, with peak sensitivity from 500 Hz to 32 kHz and pinnae amplification of 2–3x in the 2–16 kHz range.
- Smartphone electro-acoustic constraints: Standard mobile DACs (44.1/48 kHz sample rate) and micro-speakers roll off sharply above 18–20 kHz, rendering >21 kHz "ultrasonic" apps silent in practice.
- Nissan #KnockKnockCats (猫バンバン / Neko Ban Ban) & JAF (Japan Automobile Federation) field guidelines on preventing engine-bay cat fatalities, including the warning that car horns can cause cats inside engine bays to freeze or wedge deeper into belts.

## Product Principles
1. **Honest Physics Over Ultrasonic Gimmicks:** Never claim a phone speaker emits 25+ kHz ultrasound; maximize the true 13.5–19.5 kHz deliverable ceiling and combine it with biologically urgent 150 Hz – 15 kHz transients.
2. **Solve the Engine Bay Paradox:** Always distinguish between *flushing* a cat from under the car (Deter Mode) and *coaxing* a scared kitten out of a tight engine bay (Lure Mode).
3. **One-Thumb Curbside Speed:** If a driver has to hunt through menus before starting their car, they won't use it. The primary 20-second safety sweep must work from the first screen with zero setup.
4. **Humble Harm Prevention:** Deterrence must startle and annoy cats into leaving hazardous vehicle zones without causing auditory trauma to pets or bystanders.
